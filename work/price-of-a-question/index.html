The Price of a Question

I built a desk slice that answers for fractions of a cent. This is what it costs, why five models serve it, and what has to stay true for the price to hold.

LLM routing & cost Evaluation & measurement Systems architecture

30-second version. A deep research note costs $0.019 measured: one search, one Sonnet call, twelve seconds. A morning brief costs under a cent. Search is hard capped at $7.92 a month. Every number below shows the file or the arithmetic behind it.

The desk version
—

Write the full research note on Micron. Four hours of an equity research associate, at a fully loaded rate.

The measured version
—

One search, one large-model call, twelve seconds, and a report envelope that shows its own sources.

Those two numbers are not doing the same work, and this page is not going to pretend otherwise. The desk version includes judgment, a phone call, and someone whose name is on the note. The measured version does retrieval, synthesis, and a first draft. The interesting question is not which is cheaper. It is what the cheap one actually costs to keep running.

The task ledger

Price the task, not the person

Desk work splits into tasks, tasks take time, and time on a desk has a rate. Every row here shows its arithmetic.

The human column is an estimate, built the same way every time. Take a public base-comp band, load it 1.35x for what a seat costs beyond salary, and divide by a 2,600-hour desk year.

base comp band, equity research associate$130k to $160k
× 1.35 loaded: benefits, payroll tax, seat, terminal$176k to $216k
÷ desk hours a year, not the 2,080 office default2,600
fully loaded hourly$68 to $83

Run that for every seat on the desk and you get the rates the ledger uses. Midpoints, so the table stays readable.

The machine column is measured. Each row charges fixed credits set by the model that actually ran, so the price tracks real cost, not a flat guess.

Task Seat Human Surface Tier Credits Machine
Two rows cost nothing, and they are not the boring ones. Classifying whether a name is even publicly traded, and grading how a trade was exited, are both real work done with no model at all. A deterministic action is the only genuinely free thing on this list. Everything else, including the local model, draws a credit, because the local model is not free to the platform either.

The line

One request, station by station

The bill from the cold open does not arrive in one piece. It accumulates across seven stations, and only two of them cost anything.

Five of the seven stations cost nothing, and their job is to stop the other two running away. The throttle keeps a read from costing more than a write, the cache answers repeats for free, and the retrieval budget caps the context before any tokens are generated.

The router is the station worth opening. It makes its decisions in a fixed order: what the task wants, what the plan allows, what is left in the day's budget, whether the answer is already cached, and what to do if the provider is down.

The tree

What a plan actually buys

Five roles. Between each, a gate that lives in code: a model ceiling, a monthly credit budget, and a daily dollar clamp.

The paywall buys more than a bigger model. On prose tasks the router appends a depth directive for whichever tier served: free gets the core read, Pro adds forward-looking signals, Analyst adds precedent and scenarios. Same scaffold, more substance.

What it costs to serve

Move the sliders. The model runs on the real credit weights, the real per-call costs, and the real search caps.

Where the money goes, as it scales
Model calls Search Infrastructure

Model cost is a straight line, because it scales with actions. Search cost bends and then flattens: one search serves every user for the length of the cache window, so spend tracks distinct questions rather than how many people asked them. Feed, brief, and coverage on the same ticker is one paid search, verified live.

Cost per subscriber, same model

So cost per subscriber falls as the product grows. The one thing that could break that is a heavy user on an uncapped plan, which is why the daily limit is set in dollars rather than in calls: a call limit has to assume a prompt size, and a prompt near the 30,000-character ceiling costs roughly three times a typical one. Hitting the cap degrades the model instead of blocking the action.

The rig

The GPU is a development asset

There is a 5090 in the room and a vLLM profile ready for it. Here is the arithmetic on whether it should ever serve production.

Monthly cost against model calls served

Below the crossover, paying per call is cheaper, and the crossover sits a long way from where this product is. The arithmetic is not the deciding factor anyway. Cloud Run has no GPU at any memory size, so serving a local model means a second always-on machine on a home connection, carrying an availability guarantee I cannot make. The capex is the small problem.

So production runs the free tier on Haiku, and the two open-model rows drop out of the model picker when no local provider is configured — asking Anthropic for Gemma is an error, not a fallback. The free local tier is real in development and not in production, and what would change that is traffic, not a cheaper card.
