The Price of a Question

A finance desk is a machine for turning questions into answers, and every answer has a price. I built one that answers a slice of those questions for fractions of a cent. This is what the slice costs, why it takes five different models to serve it, and what has to stay true for the price to hold.

The desk version
—

Write the full research note on Micron. Four hours of an equity research associate, at a fully loaded rate.

The measured version
—

One search, one large-model call, twelve seconds, and a report envelope that shows its own sources.

Those two numbers are not doing the same work, and this page is not going to pretend otherwise. The desk version includes judgment, a phone call, and someone whose name is on the note. The measured version does retrieval, synthesis, and a first draft. The interesting question is not which is cheaper. It is what the cheap one actually costs to keep running.

The task ledger

Price the task, not the person

Nobody is replaced by a table. But desk work decomposes into tasks, tasks take time, and time on a desk has a rate. That makes every row here comparable, as long as the arithmetic is on the page.

The human column is an estimate and it is built the same way every time. Take a public base-comp band, load it for the things a seat actually costs beyond salary, and divide by a year that reflects how a desk really runs.

base comp band, equity research associate$130k to $160k
× 1.35 loaded: benefits, payroll tax, seat, terminal$176k to $216k
÷ desk hours a year, not the 2,080 office default2,600
fully loaded hourly$68 to $83

Run that for every seat on the desk and you get the rates the ledger uses. Midpoints, so the table stays readable.

The machine column is not an estimate. Each row charges a fixed number of credits, and the credit weight is set by the model that actually ran, so the price tracks the real cost rather than a flat guess.

Two rows cost nothing, and they are not the boring ones. Classifying whether a name is even publicly traded, and grading how a trade was exited, are both real work done with no model at all. A deterministic action is the only genuinely free thing on this list. Everything else, including the local model, draws a credit, because the local model is not free to the platform either.
The deck

Five models, six machines, six feeds

Every card here has a back, and the back is the interesting side. A spec sheet that only lists what something can do is marketing. The reason these choices are defensible is that the limits were measured too.

Feature code in finplatform never names a model. It names a task, and two tables decide the rest: what tier a task wants, and which model currently serves that tier. Swapping a model is a string edit in one file. That is what makes the cards below interchangeable rather than load-bearing.

The line

One request, station by station

The bill from the cold open does not arrive in one piece. It accumulates across seven stations, and only two of them cost anything.

Five of seven stations are free, and they are the ones that keep the other two from running away. The throttle stops a read from costing more than a write. The cache means a repeated question is answered without spending again. The retrieval budget caps how much context a call can carry, which caps the input bill before a single token is generated.

The router is the station worth opening. It makes six decisions in a fixed order, and the order is the whole design: what the task wants, what the plan allows, what the day's budget allows, whether the answer already exists, what to do when a provider fails, and what to tell the user if the result is not what they asked for.

The tree

What a plan actually buys

Five roles, and between each one a gate that exists in code rather than in a pricing page. A ceiling on which models you can reach, a monthly credit budget, and a daily dollar clamp that bounds the worst case.

The paywall does not just buy a bigger model. On prose tasks the router appends a depth directive keyed to the tier that actually served the request, so the free tier gets the core read, Pro gets one or two forward-looking signals, and Analyst gets historical precedent and scenario sensitivities. Same scaffold, escalating substance. That is a deliberate choice about what a subscription should feel like, and it is enforced by the same table that picks the model.

A small thing that says a lot. The file that defines all of this opens with a docstring advertising 20 credits a month for Pro and unlimited for Options. The enforced table two hundred lines below says 40 and 300. The number a user actually receives was right the entire time. Only the prose describing it drifted, which is the argument for putting invariants in code instead of in documentation.
The bill

Does the desk pay for itself

This is the number the whole page drives toward. Move the sliders: the model runs on the real credit weights, the real per-call costs, and the real search caps.

Total cost Revenue Revenue outruns cost by roughly 60x at the right edge, which is why these two need their own chart and a log axis.

The shape is the argument. Model cost is a straight line, because it scales with actions. Search cost bends and then flattens, because one search serves every user for the length of the cache window. Feed, brief, and coverage on the same ticker is exactly one paid search, verified live. Search spend tracks distinct questions per window, not how many people asked them, and it is hard capped anyway.

Cost per subscriber, same model

So the per-subscriber cost falls as the product grows, which is the good version of this curve and the reason the break-even sits so low.

One plan can go negative, and the reason is more interesting than the number. Options is sold as unlimited. Thirty large-model calls a day costs about $9.90 a month at the typical call size, which is fine against $24 of revenue. The same thirty calls carrying near the full retrieval ceiling costs about 30 calls a day × 30 days × roughly $0.030 for a prompt near the 30,000-character ceiling, which is about 3× the typical $0.011 call. This is the rate the economics notes reason with., which is not. Same user, same call count, opposite verdict.

That is why the clamp is denominated in dollars rather than in calls. A call limit would have to assume a prompt size; a dollar limit does not. Two mitigations ship: a daily spend cap that degrades the model rather than blocking the action, and a quota of 300 credits, roughly four times what a heavy user actually does.

There is also money on the table that has not been picked up. Prompt caching reads at about a tenth of input cost, is documented in the economics notes, and is not implemented. That is 20 to 40 percent of the input bill sitting unharvested, and saying so is more useful than quietly rounding the margin up.

The rig

The GPU is a development asset

There is a 5090 in the room and a vLLM profile ready for it. Here is the arithmetic on whether it should ever serve production, plotted against the two alternatives and the thing production actually does.

Monthly cost against model calls served

Below the crossover, paying per call is simply cheaper, and the crossover is a long way from where this product sits. But the arithmetic is not even the strongest argument. Cloud Run has no GPU at any memory size. Serving a local model means a second always-on machine, on a home connection, carrying an availability guarantee that does not exist. The capex is the small problem. The uptime is the real one.

Which is why production runs the free tier on Haiku, and why the two open-model rows disappear from the model picker entirely when the local provider is not configured. Asking Anthropic for Gemma is an error, not a fallback, so the option is removed rather than left to fail. "Free and open" is a development-time truth on this project, and the honest version of the case study says so.

This is an open gate, not a settled answer. The repo names the production T1 provider as an undecided launch decision. The arithmetic above says rent until roughly fifty thousand calls a month. What would change the call is not a cheaper card, it is having enough traffic that the always-on machine stops being idle most of the day.

Still open

What is not decided

    The question this page did not answer

    This priced tasks. It did not price quality. The only quality-per-dollar findings here were discovered by necessity rather than by design: a 7B model that could not hold a JSON contract, a prose path deleted because small models wrote bad analysis, and a depth ladder that makes the paywall buy visibly more words rather than provably better ones.

    What I would instrument next is the part a user can feel. Not tokens per dollar, but whether the answer at the higher tier changed what someone did. That takes a different kind of measurement than any of the tables above: paired reads on the same question, and a way to ask whether the extra depth was worth the extra charge. It is a marketing and user-research problem wearing an engineering costume, and it is the honest next chapter.

    The verdict on the part that was measured: break-even at three to six Pro subscribers, a cost curve that falls per user as it grows, and one plan whose margin depends on a clamp rather than on optimism.
