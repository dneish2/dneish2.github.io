How a 4B model beat a 27B

Models are sized in parameters. A 4B has four billion of them and fits in 3.3 GB. A 27B has twenty-seven billion and wants 17.4 GB, which is more than most laptops will hand over. I tested seven on the same translation work expecting the big one to win, and it came sixth. Here is what I measured, plus two other things that surprised me enough to change the code.

David Neish

Passage translates text, documents, photos and speech, and it runs on whatever model is reachable: something local, your own endpoint, or a hosted one. Which meant I had to find out which model to actually use.

1. The smallest model won

Every model got the same sentences to translate, and I already had a known-good translation of each one. The score below is how often a model's answer lined up with that known-good version. Higher is better, 1.0 would be perfect agreement, and nothing real gets close to that.

The smallest model in the test won. The one it beat most clearly was the 27B from its own family: five times the size, worse agreement, and nearly twice as slow to answer.

translategemma:4b 0.788 3.3 GB · 368 ms
gemma3:12b 0.787 8.2 GB · 582 ms
translategemma:12b 0.779 8.1 GB · 495 ms
qwen2.5:7b 0.763 4.7 GB · 237 ms
gemma4:latest 0.741 9.6 GB · 398 ms
translategemma:27b 0.734 17.4 GB · 679 ms
gemma3:1b 0.668 0.8 GB · not usable

Bars start at 0.65 so the spread is visible. Highlighted: the 4B that won, and the 27B of the same family it beat.

The top three are within a hair of each other. What separates them is cost: the winner is a third the size of its nearest rival and a fifth the size of the 27B, and it is the one that runs comfortably on a laptop. Being built for one job beat being big at it.

2. Six of seven models ignored an instruction

Some things in a translation have to survive untouched. URLs, email addresses, figures, currency. The obvious move is to tell the model to leave them alone. I did, explicitly, and then checked.

6 of 7 rewrote something they were told not to touch

So the spans are pulled out before the text ever reaches a model, and put back afterwards. The model never sees them and cannot damage them. The same swap doubles as the injection defence: an instruction hidden in the source gets translated literally instead of obeyed, because by the time a model sees it, it is just text.

This is the part I would keep from the whole project. An instruction in a prompt is a request. If something must hold every time, it has to be built into the code path, not asked for.

3. A reasoning model spent 8,786 characters saying nothing

The qwen3 family looked broken: empty responses, every time. The answers were going to a reasoning channel that the compatibility endpoint I was using does not expose. Through the native API the text arrived, which is how I found the real problem.

It had spent 8,786 characters deliberating a one-line translation, then produced no translation. That is not a setting to tune. Translation is transduction, not deduction, and a model that reasons about the answer is solving a different problem than the one in front of it. Thinking models stayed selectable and came out of the default roster.

What I would tell you

Match the model to the shape of the job before you touch anything else. The smallest model built for the task beat a general model two and a half times its size, and a model built for reasoning could not do the task at all. That decision was worth more than every prompt I wrote.

And anything that must be true every time belongs in code. Prompts are a good way to shape an answer, and a poor way to guarantee one.
